When mutual funds face large redemptions, they sell bonds. Those sales should depress prices and impair liquidity—but do they? Our research shows that the answer depends almost entirely on **whether dealer balance sheets have capacity to absorb the selling pressure**.

## The Conditional Fire Sale Effect

We study **60 mutual funds, 200 corporate bonds, in a monthly panel**, focusing on the March 2020 episode when redemption pressures peaked. Using the **MFITP (mutual fund implied trading pressure) instrumental variable** to isolate exogenous selling, we find a striking result:

The **standalone redemption effect on bond liquidity is statistically insignificant**. On its own, mutual fund selling does not reliably impair market quality. This challenges the conventional narrative that fire sales are inherently destabilizing.

Fire sales impair liquidity **only when dealer balance sheets are already constrained**. The conditional effect is **13.3 basis points (t = 2.9)**—economically meaningful and statistically robust. In other words, mutual fund redemptions are a necessary but not sufficient condition for fire-sale externalities. The binding constraint is dealer intermediation capacity.

## Why Dealer Capacity Matters

The mechanism is straightforward. When dealers have ample balance sheet capacity, they absorb mutual fund selling with minimal price impact. Inventory accumulates temporarily, but dealers can warehouse the bonds and redistribute them to natural buyers over time. Prices remain close to fundamental values.

When dealer balance sheets are constrained—by risk limits, capital requirements, or repo financing stress—dealers cannot absorb the selling. They either widen spreads dramatically or refuse to quote altogether. The result is a **liquidity vacuum** where mutual fund selling translates directly into price dislocations.

## March 2020 as a Natural Experiment

The March 2020 episode provides a clean test because redemption pressures were large and sudden, and dealer constraints varied across bonds and over time. Bonds held by funds with high redemption rates experienced significant liquidity deterioration—but only when the primary dealers for those bonds were simultaneously facing balance sheet pressure from other sources.

## Policy Implications

Our findings shift the policy focus from **preventing redemptions** (through swing pricing, gates, or redemption fees) to **maintaining dealer capacity**. If fire-sale externalities are conditional on dealer constraints, then policies that preserve or expand intermediation capacity during stress—countercyclical capital relief, standing repo facilities, or temporary market-maker support programs—are more effective than trying to slow the outflow from mutual funds. The constraint is on the supply side of liquidity, not the demand side.
